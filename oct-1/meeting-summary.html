Meeting summary: Thu Oct 1

ISBA 4796, week 5, Hilton 106. Discovery and stakeholder engagement.

Overview

This session asked one question: how do you know you're solving the right problem? Every capstone project drifts from its first plan, and the most common failure is building the wrong thing because nobody understood the problem first. Stakeholder discovery is how you find out before you build. We practiced it with a four-step interview, a live demo, and interviews in small groups.

Why discovery comes first

Andrew Presa wants to build a mentorship app that connects students looking for jobs with peers who've completed internships and received return offers. My advice to him was to listen to students before committing to an app or a budget. That's the same advice for your capstone. A stakeholder will often bring you a solution, like a chatbot, a dashboard, or an ML model. Your first job is to understand the process and the problem behind the request.

Example, explore, reflect, next

  1. Example: ask for a real story with an open question, like "Tell me about your internship." Build rapport first and let them talk.
  2. Explore: follow up on what they said and pull on the threads. 5W2H helps here: who, what, when, where, why, how, and how much. Don't lead them toward an answer.
  3. Reflect: play back what you heard in your own words. They'll correct you or add something, and you explore again.
  4. Next: ask what you haven't asked about, who else you should talk to, and what should happen next.

Think of it as a funnel. You start broad to find threads worth following, then get specific. It's the same move as going from descriptive to diagnostic analytics: first what happened, then why.

The demo interview

I interviewed a classmate who had an internship that led to a return offer. Two of the most useful details came out only after I played back what I'd heard. One was how a student organization led to the referral, and the other was how much AI helped with case interview prep. Neither came from a direct question. Both came out because I checked my understanding out loud.

Practice in groups

Groups of about four interviewed a classmate who'd been through an internship. Each person had a role:

RoleWhat you do
FacilitatorOpens the conversation with a broad question. Doesn't take notes, so they can listen.
ListenerAsks at least one follow-up based on what was said.
Note-takerCaptures keywords, names, and dates, and reflects back a summary.
SkepticNames at least one assumption made during the interview. Hold it first and see if the interview answers it on its own.

In the debrief, the most common miss was asking specific questions too early, before the story was out. The best openings were plain, like "Tell me about your internship," with no detail that leads the answer. Reflecting back kept surfacing details that hadn't come up yet.

Across the stories we heard, a person made the connection far more often than a cold application did. It was a student organization, a campus job, a teammate, or a class portfolio on GitHub that a hiring manager saw. Andrew can use that finding when he designs the program.

Applying it to a business

Most business requests come down to increasing revenue or decreasing cost. Either way, turn the general goal into a request for a story.

The stakeholder saysYou ask instead
"How do we increase sales?""Walk me through the last customer you expected to buy but didn't."
"How can we automate this?""Walk me through the last time this process took more time, effort, or resources than expected."

Then run the same four steps. Your first capstone milestone is to identify and frame the problem correctly before you decide what to build.

AI as interview help

I showed my Granola notes from the session. An AI notetaker records the transcript, and the scratch notes you type during the meeting tell it what mattered. Writing keywords, names, and dates by hand still helps you focus and reflect. AI is also useful before and after a meeting: to prep questions, suggest follow-ups, and debrief what you heard. For DAMA Days, you could have Claude research the speakers and open roles, then prepare a few targeted questions.

Key takeaways

  • Stakeholder discovery is getting someone to talk about a real situation, listening, asking follow-up questions, and using what you learn to frame the problem and decide what to do next.
  • It isn't building a solution right away.
  • Ask for a story, not an opinion, and let the last answer pick your next question.
  • Play back what you heard. It catches your mistakes and brings out details nobody asked about.
